If you look after someone: money, respite, and help for you

Most of the care in this country is given by family members who are not paid, not trained, and not asked how they are coping. There is more help than you have been told about — some of it money, some of it time off. This page is for you, not for the person you care for.

Checked on 3 September 2026

At a glance

What you may get
Paid leave from work through Employment Insurance, a tax credit worth up to $8,601 in claimable amount, respite so you can rest, and free help at home.
Who it is for
Anyone looking after a family member, partner or friend who is ill, disabled or frail. You do not have to live together, and you do not have to be paid.
What it costs
Nothing to ask. Home support is free for people on GIS or assistance; other services charge by income.
The one thing to do first
Phone Island Health on 1-888-533-2273 and ask for an assessment. You do not need a doctor's referral, and you can make the call yourself.

You probably are a caregiver, even if you do not use the word

People rarely call themselves caregivers. They say "I just help my mum", or "he's my husband, of course I do it".

But the programs on this page use the word, and if you do not use it too, you will not be offered them. If you regularly help someone with meals, washing, medicine, appointments, money or getting around because of their age, illness or disability, you are a caregiver. Say that word when you phone.

You do not have to live together

None of the programs below require you to live in the same home as the person you care for. Several do not even require you to be related.

Paid time off work: EI caregiving benefits

If caring for someone means you cannot work, Employment Insurance may pay you while you do it. There are three kinds, and which one applies depends on who is ill and how ill they are.

EI caregiving benefits, 2026
Benefit Who it is for How many weeks
Family caregiver benefit for children A critically ill or injured child under 18 Up to 35 weeks
Family caregiver benefit for adults A critically ill or injured person 18 or older Up to 15 weeks
Compassionate care benefits Someone needing end-of-life care, child or adult Up to 26 weeks

All three pay 55% of your earnings, up to $729 a week for 2026.

To qualify you generally need:

  • 600 insured hours of work in the 52 weeks before your claim (or since your last claim);
  • your weekly earnings to have dropped by more than 40% for at least one week because of the caring;
  • a medical certificate from a doctor or nurse practitioner saying the person is critically ill, injured, or needs end-of-life care.

You may not have to be family

You can qualify as a family member or as someone "considered to be like a family member". In that case the person you care for, or their legal representative, signs a short attestation form saying so. Friends, neighbours and chosen family are not automatically shut out.

Self-employed people can qualify too, through the EI special benefits program for the self-employed, if their net self-employed earnings in the previous calendar year were at least $9,254.

What to say when you phone Service Canada

"I need to stop working to care for a family member who is seriously ill. I want to apply for EI caregiving benefits. Can you tell me which of the three applies to my situation, and what the doctor needs to sign?"

The Canada Caregiver Credit

This is a tax credit, not a cheque. It reduces the income tax you owe.

You can claim it if you support a spouse or partner with a mental or physical infirmity, or a child, grandchild, parent, grandparent, brother, sister, aunt, uncle, niece or nephew — yours or your partner's — who depends on you for food, shelter or clothing because of that infirmity.

For the 2025 tax year the amounts are $2,687 on lines 30300, 30400 or 30500, and up to $8,601 on line 30425 or 30450. The $8,601 amount for an adult dependant shrinks as their net income rises, and disappears once it reaches $28,798.

The CRA may ask for a signed note from a medical practitioner saying when the infirmity began and how long it is expected to last — unless the person already has an approved Disability Tax Credit, in which case that does the job.

You must file to get it

A credit is claimed on a tax return. If you have not filed, you cannot claim it — and the free clinics will help you file, including past years. See our guide to free tax help.

The credit you can move onto your own return

This one is quietly worth a lot, and almost nobody is told about it.

If the person you care for is approved for the Disability Tax Credit but has too little income to use all of it, the unused part can be transferred to a supporting spouse, parent, child or other close relative — which may be you. It goes on line 31800 if it comes from a dependant, or line 32600 if from a spouse.

So it is worth helping the person you care for get approved for the Disability Tax Credit even if they pay no tax at all. The credit does not go to waste; it can come to you instead.

Respite: getting a break

Respite means someone else takes over for a while — a few hours, a day, or a short stay in a care home — so you can sleep, work, or simply stop.

Through Island Health you can ask about:

  • Adult day programs — the person spends the day somewhere with activities and care. Up to $10 a day.
  • Short-stay respite beds — a temporary stay in a care home. $49.57 a day in 2026.
  • Home support — a worker comes to the house.

Ask about respite at the same time as you ask about care for the person you look after. It is a separate conversation and it will not usually be offered unless you raise it.

Do not wait until you are at breaking point

Assessments and waiting lists take time. The moment you first think "I cannot keep doing this", make the call — not weeks later. Rates can also be reduced for hardship, so ask about a hardship reduction if the daily rate is out of reach rather than going without.

If the person has a developmental disability

Community Living BC (CLBC) funds supported living, respite for families, employment support and skill development for adults 19 and over with a developmental disability — and, through the Personalized Supports Initiative, for people diagnosed with Autism Spectrum Disorder or Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder who need a lot of help with daily tasks.

CLBC does not send you a cheque. It pays for services. Respite paid through an agency is one of them, so ask specifically about respite when you speak to a facilitator.

Phone 1-877-660-2522. You can start the process before the person turns 19, and it is worth doing — assessments take time.

Why people miss this

  • They do not call themselves a caregiver, so they never search for caregiver programs and are never offered them.
  • They think help is only for the sick person. Respite exists for the carer. So does EI.
  • They assume they earn too much, or too little. The tax credit has no minimum, and home support is free on GIS or assistance.
  • They wait to be offered a break. Nobody offers. You have to ask, in those words.
  • They do not file a tax return, so the caregiver credit and any transferred disability amount are lost.
  • They do not know the Disability Tax Credit can transfer to them, so nobody applies for it when the ill person pays no tax.

Looking after yourself is not optional

Caregivers get ill. They lose jobs, savings and sleep, and they are more likely to end up needing care themselves. None of that is a character failing — it is what happens when one person absorbs a job that services were supposed to do.

Two things worth saying plainly:

  • Asking for respite is not giving up on someone. It is how people keep going for years instead of months.
  • If you are not coping, say so out loud to someone official — the Island Health case manager, your doctor, the Seniors Advocate. "I am not coping" is a sentence that changes what gets offered.

If you are in crisis, call or text 9-8-8 at any hour. If things are hard but not an emergency, dial 211 and say you are a caregiver and you need support.
